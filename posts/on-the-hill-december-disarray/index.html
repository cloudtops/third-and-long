On the Hill

December Disarray

By Henry Houghton/Week 10

Well folks, we’ve made it. Think back to those long summer months, sweating through the heat and outlasting a seemingly endless sports drought. All you could dream of was a day when the air turned crisp, the leaves started falling, and your favorite team just beat their rival. Ladies (hopefully) and gentlemen (mainly), that day is here. Once again, what an action-packed weekend of college football. Utah dominated the second half in a top-10 win against BYU in the Holy War. Notre Dame held down their home turf and took down #1 Miami in a statement win. Georgia asserted their dominance against Ole Miss and proved why they’re the top team in the land. Tennessee gave Kentucky a signature Rocky Top experience and proved once again why their defense should be taken very seriously. Texas marched into Missouri and took care of business, adding another impressive win to their resume. And saving the best for last, we watched the Alabama Crimson Tide travel to Baton Rouge as 6.5 point underdogs and put belt to Tiger ass.

On Tuesday night, the CFB Playoff held their weekly rankings show and stirred things up a bit more as we drift into the thick of the season. Like I said last week, don’t take these rankings too seriously yet. We have three more weeks of madness before those rankings will have any real meaning. What I’m really worried about is the announcement of the playoff being expanded from 12 to 24 teams and the elimination of conference championship games starting next year. Anything to put more money in ESPN’s pocket. I feel like 12 teams in the playoff is already too many… now we’re at 24? I was more than happy with the 4-team playoff format. It rewarded the four best teams but also maintained the importance of regular season games. Will there be 4-loss teams in the new playoff format? You cannot seriously tell me there are 24 teams in the country who have an actual shot at winning the national championship. Maybe I’ll be proven wrong with a Cinderella run and a new football version of March Madness. December Disarray, if you will?

This week, we have Power 4 coaches taking the podium and questioning the point of even having conferences without a championship to play for. I completely understand where they’re coming from. Maybe being independent like Notre Dame is the advantage after all? Having the autonomy to customize your schedule without conference scheduling restrictions sounds pretty ideal. Geographically, these conferences don’t even make sense anymore. Stanford and Cal in the ATLANTIC Coast Conference? The Big Ten has teams spread from Oregon to Maryland. It makes no sense. Unfortunately, proximity is the least important factor for any of these major conferences. The locations of the schools don’t actually matter. Programs benefit from conference membership in a lot of ways other than just being close to each other.

For starters, the top ranked team of each Power 4 conference gets an automatic bid into the new playoff. Sure, that is a pretty nice incentive, but in highly-competitive conferences, the majority of teams obviously won’t benefit from this. What is the point of scheduling a bunch of powerhouse in-conference programs if finishing anywhere but first place means nothing? You can still get a playoff bid at-large and recent rankings suggest that the committee is considering strength of schedule. So there is still at least a slight incentive to continue playing in conferences by simply being associated with other strong programs. Being provided an automatic pathway would be great, but I think acquiring one of the remaining 19 bids at large (1 automatic bid goes to the highest Group of Six Champion) is still very achievable in a strong conference.

The next thing to consider (and hardest to escape) is the licensing and broadcasting deals that major conferences have with major networks. Not only would team viewership rights become a complete mess, but schools would also be forced to handle scheduling on their own now. Conference headquarters and staff provide a sneaky important aspect to everything that happens in college athletics. I think schools would much rather rely on their conference HQ to handle all of the logistics and busy work rather than doing it all on their own. It’s hard to say at this point. Some schools and athletic directors may feel that leaving a conference and having the flexibility to schedule their own unique opponents would give their team a better chance to play in the postseason. Maybe the increase in work for universities is worth the trouble? However, the point remains regarding television rights and contracts; it is going to take some serious navigation for a Power 4 team to transition to Independent.

Lastly, it is very important to consider that football is not the only college sport and conferences are pretty consistent across the board. As much as I’d like football to dictate all of their decision making, I still really like basketball, baseball, soccer, etc. For many schools, their basketball teams generate way more wealth than their football teams. You can’t just screw over the other sports teams because the college football landscape negatively impacted your football team. Regardless of the uncertainty, major conference realignment or even the dissolution of conferences is going to be quite the process. I don’t think it can happen over one off season. With that being said, we just saw the legendary Pac-12 go to the grave in a hurry. So maybe it can happen overnight…

We are truly entering uncharted territory; at least in my lifetime. I don’t know what the future is going to hold, but I get the feeling that the drama is just getting started. The way coaches are talking about this new playoff and conference structure doesn’t sound like they are too thrilled with the decision. With 24 teams in each postseason now, I can see some programs taking an unconventional approach in the coming years. I’m not exactly sure what “unconventional” is going to look like, but I’d bet my money on things continuing to change dramatically over the next five years.
