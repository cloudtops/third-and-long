Purgatory in the Palouse

How did Washington State get here, and how does it get out?

By Noah Labao/Week 8

Washington State has never been a blue blood, and it has never needed to be. For most of the last three decades, the Cougars were a team that could win the conference. They won Pac-10 titles in 1997 and 2002, and the 1997 team went all the way to Pasadena to face No. 1 Michigan in the Rose Bowl. In 2018 they were a hair away from clinching the Pac-12 North. They were also the team nobody wanted on the schedule. Visitors flew into a regional airport, drove past miles of wheat and walked into a 35,000-seat stadium where the wind and the cold did half the work. A good team could show up in Pullman expecting a win and leave with its season in pieces. For a generation, Washington State was the trap game everyone else circled in red. Now, at 1-7 and winless in a rebuilt Pac-12, fresh off a home loss to Boise State, the Cougars are the ones getting circled as an easy win. How did we get here?

The short version starts with Mike Leach. His Air Raid turned Pullman into appointment television, and it peaked in 2018, when Gardner Minshew threw for a Pac-12-record 4,779 yards and the Cougars went a school-record 11-2 and finished tenth in the country. Leach left for Mississippi State after the 2019 season, and the program hasn’t found steady footing since. Nick Rolovich got a pandemic-shortened season and seven more games before he was fired in October 2021 for refusing the state’s vaccine mandate. Jake Dickert steadied things at 7-6, 5-7 and 8-4 while realignment emptied the Pac-12 until only Washington State and Oregon State were left.

December 2024: Quarterback John Mateer, coming off a breakout year that powered an 8-1 start, left for Oklahoma, and days later Dickert left for Wake Forest before the bowl game. Jimmy Rogers, fresh off an unbeaten FCS title at South Dakota State, rebuilt the roster on the fly, went 6-6 and left for Iowa State a year later. Now it’s Kirby Moore, a first-time head coach who has beaten only FCS Duquesne through eight games. That’s four full-time coaches since 2019, and every exit took players and momentum out the door with it. The losing is a symptom. The pattern is the problem. So what does it take to break it?

None of this is a death sentence. Washington State still has things most programs in its spot would envy: a fan base that keeps making the drive, a brand that still shows up on national broadcasts, a conference it can realistically win, and a head coach who actually wants to be in Pullman. What it doesn’t have is a structure that lets any of that last. Fixing that starts with money, because in this era everything does.

Schools can now pay players directly and the biggest departments are spending as fast as the rules allow. Washington State isn’t going to win a bidding war with Oklahoma for a five-star recruit, and we shouldn’t try. Its edge has always been finding the overlooked 2 or 3 Star and turning him into a pro prospect. The problem is that the portal now lets richer programs collect those players as soon as they break out, which is exactly what happened when Mateer left amid reports of a seven-figure offer. The smartest use of every NIL and revenue-sharing dollar in Pullman is retention: paying to keep the quarterback, the left tackle and the breakout receiver the staff already developed, so a good season becomes the start of something instead of the end of it.

That money has to come from somewhere, and Washington State has more of it in its alumni base than its football budget suggests. Jamie Kern Lima graduated summa cum laude from WSU and later sold IT Cosmetics to L’Oréal for $1.2 billion. Edmund Schweitzer, the WSU-trained engineer behind Pullman-based Schweitzer Engineering Laboratories, has already given tens of millions to the university with his wife and his company. Kurt Beecher Dammeier, the founder of Beecher’s Handmade Cheese, traces his love of food to discovering Cougar Gold in Pullman. Sean Tresvant earned two degrees at WSU before running marketing for the Jordan Brand and becoming CEO of Taco Bell. Paul Allen was a WSU Regents Distinguished Alumnus whose name is on the School for Global Animal Health, though reaching that estate for athletics is a long shot. Almost none of that goodwill has ever been pointed at football. The athletic department needs to go after it on purpose, with a real collective, a seat at the table for the people writing checks, and a clear plan for what their money buys. Pitched as an investment in the school’s identity rather than a bailout, it’s a far easier sell.

Then there’s the sideline. If Moore keeps underwhelming, there are two roads. The first is to move on quickly and chase a splash hire to wake up a tired fan base. But the pool of coaches with real buzz who would choose a smaller-money league is thin, and the last three hires proved that anyone good enough to win in Pullman is good enough to be hired away from it. A quick trigger would also make it five head coaches in eight seasons. The second road is patience, and it’s probably the smarter one. Moore took over a roster rebuilt on the fly, in a brand-new conference, in his first year running a program. A real third season, judged on clear benchmarks like bowl eligibility, a competitive Pac-12 record and a portal class he manages to keep, would give the school a fair answer instead of a panicked one. Whoever the coach is, the contract matters as much as the hire. Buyouts that make leaving expensive and an assistant salary pool that keeps good coordinators in town are how a stepping stone becomes a destination.

The plan on the field should play to what made Washington State dangerous in the first place. Moore built his reputation calling plays at Fresno State and Missouri, and an aggressive, recognizable offense gives a quarterback a reason to pick eastern Washington over a bigger name. The recruiting map should start at home. Moore grew up in Prosser, about two and a half hours from campus, and he can sell Washington, Idaho and Oregon kids on staying close to home and being the face of a comeback, a pitch no out-of-region power can match. In the portal, the target isn’t the most expensive player available. It’s the overlooked one who wants a starting job and a place to prove people wrong, the same profile Minshew fit in 2018.

Finally, Washington State has to win the league it actually plays in. The rebuilt Pac-12, with Boise State, Fresno State, San Diego State and Colorado State, isn’t the conference of the Rose Bowl era, but a title in it is the clearest path back to national relevance and to a seat at the table when realignment comes around again. That run starts at home. Martin Stadium was once the place good teams dreaded visiting, and making Saturdays in Pullman feel that way again, packed, loud and miserable for the other side, is where every rebuild begins.

Purgatory, after all, isn’t the end of the story. It’s the waiting room. The fans still make the drive and ‘Ole Crimson still shows up on ESPN set after set; so if you build it, we’ll come. What Washington State hasn’t had since Leach left is the one thing that cost nothing back then and costs almost everything now: time.
